Glossary

Each word with what it means, an everyday example, and what it cannot prove alone.

TER — the fund’s annual fee+

It is the percentage charged each year to operate the fund. It directly reduces what the investor keeps.

Example: A 0.25% TER costs about $25 a year on $10,000 invested, before investment returns.

Limit: The fund does not always show a separate bill: the fee is generally deducted gradually from its value.

Assets under management — fund size+

It is the total value of money and investments managed by the fund.

Example: Like the size of the shared money pot entrusted to the manager.

Limit: A large fund is not automatically better; also check fees and holdings.

Beta — movement compared with the market+

It estimates whether a stock usually moves more or less than the market.

Example: Beta of 1.5: when the market moved 2%, the stock often moved about 3% either way.

Limit: It measures the past; it does not promise the next move.

Debt compared with equity+

It compares borrowed money with the money that truly belongs to shareholders.

Example: 1.5 means about $150 of debt for $100 of equity.

Limit: Normal levels differ by sector; a bank and a store should not be compared the same way.

Bear case — what could go wrong+

It describes a possible future where the company faces difficulties.

Example: Costs rise while customers buy less.

Limit: This scenario helps you prepare; it does not predict the future.

Dividend — money paid to shareholders+

Some companies share part of their profits with people who own their shares.

Example: $0.25 paid four times a year gives $1 per share annually.

Limit: A dividend can be reduced or stopped; it is never guaranteed.

EPS — earnings per share+

It is the part of total profit that belongs to one share.

Example: $100 of profit across 20 shares gives EPS of $5.

Limit: Look across several years: one good quarter can be exceptional.

ETF — exchange-traded fund+

It is a basket that can hold many stocks, bonds, or other investments.

Example: Like buying a box of many colored pencils instead of one pencil.

Limit: A basket can still be concentrated in a few companies or one sector.

Catalyst — event that may change the situation+

It is an expected event that may quickly change market opinion in either direction.

Example: Quarterly results, a court decision, or a product launch.

Limit: The event may disappoint or already be reflected in the price.

Bull case — what could go well+

It describes a possible future where sales, profit, or conditions improve.

Example: Demand rises and the company produces enough to meet it.

Limit: It is a hypothesis to test, not a promise.

Free cash flow (cash left over)+

It is the cash left after the spending needed to run the business.

Example: Like your pocket money after paying for transport and lunch.

Limit: A positive amount helps, but check whether it repeats over time.

Health score (out of 100)+

One score that groups several company signals. 53 out of 100 means “middle of the road,” not “good buy.”

Example: Like a school report: the average combines several subjects, but you must open the details to see strengths and weaknesses.

Limit: This score is not a probability of profit and is not enough on its own to justify an alert.

Liquidity — ease of buying or selling+

A liquid investment usually finds a buyer or seller quickly without a large price gap.

Example: A bottle of water is easier to resell than a rare custom-made object.

Limit: Low liquidity can make the displayed price hard to obtain in practice.

MACD — comparison of two trends+

It compares a fast and a slow average to show whether movement is gaining or losing strength.

Example: Like comparing a fast bicycle with a slow one to see which is pulling ahead.

Limit: The signal can arrive after the move and should be read with price and volume.

Market capitalization — total market value+

It is one share’s price multiplied by the number of shares.

Example: 10 million shares at $20 give a market value of $200 million.

Limit: This is not cash in the company’s bank account.

NAV — net asset value+

It is the calculated value of everything the fund owns, minus what it owes.

Example: If the basket is worth $1,000 and has 100 units, NAV is about $10 per unit.

Limit: NAV may be published later than the price shown on the exchange.

Net margin (share of sales kept)+

It shows how much profit remains after all expenses.

Example: A 12% margin: about $12 remains as profit from $100 of sales.

Limit: Compare it mainly with companies in the same business.

OBV — cumulative volume by price direction+

It adds or subtracts volume depending on whether price rises or falls.

Example: Like counting people entering a stadium and subtracting those leaving.

Limit: It helps confirm a move; alone, it does not say what to do.

P/E — price divided by earnings+

It compares a share’s price with the profit the company earns for that share.

Example: P/E of 20: the market pays about $20 for $1 of annual profit.

Limit: A low number is not always a bargain; the company may also have problems.

Risk-on / risk-off — mood toward risk+

It summarizes whether investors currently prefer risk or caution.

Example: Like choosing between playing outside in the rain or staying sheltered.

Limit: Mood can change quickly and does not make every stock the same.

ROE — return on equity+

It shows how much the company earns with shareholders’ money.

Example: ROE of 15%: $100 of equity produced about $15 of profit.

Limit: Heavy debt can inflate this number; check debt too.

RSI — relative strength index+

A gauge from 0 to 100 that measures the speed and strength of recent price moves.

Example: Like a runner who sped up quickly: they may continue or slow down; the gauge cannot decide for them.

Limit: Above 70 or below 30 does not automatically mean sell or buy.

SMA — simple moving average+

It smooths recent prices to make the direction easier to see.

Example: SMA 20 = average of the last 20 closing prices, like the average of 20 grades.

Limit: It looks backward and reacts late; it is not a prediction.

Volume — number of shares traded+

It counts shares that changed hands during a period.

Example: Like the number of people using a checkout: a larger number means more activity.

Limit: High volume says something is happening, not whether the news is good or bad.

Performance since the start of the year+

It measures the change between January 1 and today.

Example: From $100 in January to $108 today: about +8% year to date.

Limit: It restarts every year and does not show the investment’s full history.
